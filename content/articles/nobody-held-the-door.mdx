There was a day you stopped being a boy. You probably could not name it now. It was not a ceremony. It was a bedroom with the door closed, or a parking lot after a phone call, or the first morning of a job where no one was coming to help you. Something ended that day, and something else started, and you crossed between them alone. No one stood at that line. No one looked at you and said, out loud, this is real, you have changed, we see it. You just kept walking. You have been walking ever since.

That is not a wound with a story attached. It is quieter than that. It is a threshold you passed with no one holding it.

## What initiation actually does

Initiation is not a hard weekend. It is not discomfort you buy and survive. Strip away the smoke and the drums and here is the mechanism, plain: an older man who knows what is happening stands at the threshold with you and witnesses the crossing. He marks it. He says, in a way your body believes, you are not who you were, and that is real.

<PullQuote>Ceremony is only the container. The elder is the thing that works.</PullQuote>

Take it away and the crossing still happens. The job, the marriage, the loss, the burial. Your body ages on schedule. What does not update is the internal accounting. You cross into a man's life carrying a boy's ledger, and no one ever tells the ledger the boy is gone. Most men I meet have been running that arithmetic for decades without noticing they never closed it out.

## Two ways a man arrives here

Two men tend to find this. The first one built. He stayed when others left, held a marriage, a craft, a house other people could stand inside of. He endured, which is a plain word for something most people cannot do. He has lost things and made no show of it. You will not find him weeping in the road. You will find him the next morning, up early, tending what is still alive, the grief gone underground where the roots are.

The second man moved. He was fast, and the speed was the point, because as long as he kept moving the thing behind him never sat down across from him. He has burned bridges other people still talk about and lit fires that warmed strangers.

Different doors. Same room. Both men arrive holding the same question, whether or not they can say it yet: What have I actually crossed, and who was there to see.

## Where I learned this

For me it was thirty years. Celtic ceremony first, then twenty years in the Native American Church. Traditions out of Central and South America that do not translate cleanly and were never meant to. The depth psychology lineages alongside all of it: Jungian, transpersonal, the ones that map the same country from the other side. I did not collect these. You do not collect a thing that changes you.

A man learns from where the teaching actually lives, not from one tradition's brochure.

I say this so you know whose hands you would be in. Then I set it down.

## What happens at the retreats

Here is the work itself. We go out. Wilderness, real ground, the body all the way in it, not a lodge with a fire feature. You make fire from a flint, and the flint is not a symbol of anything. It is a flint. It works or your hands were wrong, and the cold does not care about your intentions. That is the difference I want you to feel. The stakes are actual. Nothing here is a metaphor pretending to be an experience.

There is ceremony, and it holds the moment the way ceremony is built to. And there are men around you doing their own crossing at the same time, in the same dirt, none of them performing invulnerability. A men's weekend almost never has that. Nobody is proving he is fine. Everybody arrived because he is not, quite, and stopped pretending at the trailhead. You do the crossing in company, witnessed, the way it was always meant to be done.

## The door is here

I do not fix men. I hold a threshold. That is a different job and an older one, and it is the only one I know how to do honestly.
